User Research

Methods for understanding learner needs and testing usability in practice[cite: 1]

Identifying User Needs

User research identifies specific points of friction in academic workflows[cite: 1]. Rather than assuming what learners or educators require, inquiry begins by examining daily tasks, time constraints, and instructional difficulties[cite: 1].

Inquiry Methods

Multiple qualitative and quantitative inquiry methods are applied depending on the project phase[cite: 1]:

  • Surveys: Structured questionnaires measure user perceptions and self-reported task completion across cohorts[cite: 1].
  • Interviews: Semi-structured discussions provide detailed accounts of how staff and students approach specific academic tasks[cite: 1].
  • Focus Groups: Group conversations highlight shared difficulties and differing perspectives between learners and tutors[cite: 1].
  • Observation: Direct observation of users working with software interfaces identifies unstated usability problems[cite: 1].
  • Open-text Feedback: Free-text comments reveal specific edge cases and unanticipated system behaviours[cite: 1].

Usability Testing and Accessibility

Usability testing verifies whether users can navigate interfaces cleanly and complete required tasks without confusion[cite: 1]. Accessibility checks ensure compatibility with assistive technologies such as screen readers[cite: 1].

In accessibility-focused projects like BEAT and VoiceCrafter, early testing checked whether controls were understandable and whether generated outputs met user requirements[cite: 1].

Translating Evidence into Product Decisions

Collected feedback is analysed thematically and translated into concrete product requirements[cite: 1]. Identified issues are prioritised by their impact on the learning task, directly informing software iterations[cite: 1].
